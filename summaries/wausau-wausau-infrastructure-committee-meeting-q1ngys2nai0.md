# Wausau Infrastructure Committee Meeting

**Organization:** City of Wausau

**Summarized:** 2026-11-21 17:10 UTC

## Meeting Overview
The Wausau Infrastructure and Facilities Committee held its September 10, 2026 meeting with heavy public participation focused on the city's controversial change to boulevard snow plowing practices. Seventeen members of the public spoke urging a return to plowing snow toward center boulevards rather than to the curb, citing safety hazards, blocked driveways, and difficulty accessing emergency services. The committee discussed the issue at length, directed city staff and the city attorney to research the legal feasibility of plowing against traffic under a Wisconsin statute, and took action on a boulevard landscape planting subcommittee; no final vote on the plowing policy was taken as the item was listed for discussion only.

## Key Discussions
### Public Comment — Boulevard Snow Plowing
Seventeen residents spoke during public comment, nearly all urging the city to return to its historical practice of plowing snow toward the center boulevard rather than to the curb. Speakers cited blocked driveways, inability to park, safety hazards for elderly and disabled neighbors, impeded emergency vehicle access, and inability to reach sand barrels. Lou Larson, Alderperson for District 10, stated that the city attorney's office and the city's insurer confirmed that a Wisconsin statute (346.05 sub-2) permits plows to operate against traffic for highway maintenance with proper lighting, and argued no new equipment costing $40,000 was necessary. Carol Lukens, Alderperson for District 1, corroborated this legal finding and argued the specific boulevard plowing change was implemented without proper council approval or resident notice. One resident presented a detailed data analysis document arguing that returning to center-boulevard plowing would save the city tens of thousands of dollars annually compared to current obligations such as clearing median sidewalks.

### Item 4: Boulevard Landscape Planting Subcommittee
Alderperson Andrew Wiskowski moved to establish a special exploratory subcommittee to develop a boulevard planting scheme addressing what plantings — such as native plants or pollinator gardens — would be permissible in the boulevard right-of-way. The motion was amended, accepted as a friendly amendment, to require the subcommittee to work in cooperation with the sustainability commission. City engineering staff noted concerns about sight lines near intersections and excavation permits for utilities that could disrupt plantings. The amended motion was approved by voice vote.

### Item 5A: Snowplowing Policy Discussion
Committee members discussed the boulevard plowing controversy at length. Alderperson Kristin Slonski and Alderperson Matt Hoenecke both thanked residents for their engagement and said the revelations about the Wisconsin statute had shifted their views. Director of Public Works Lynman explained that the city had been advised years ago it could not plow against traffic without closing the road, and that vendor estimates for a left-wing plow attachment were approximately $15,000–$20,000 for equipment plus a similar amount for hydraulics, though nothing was provided in writing. The committee directed the city attorney to provide a formal, researched opinion on the legal requirements for plowing against traffic under state statute and any associated driver liability questions, and directed staff to return at a future meeting with costed options for how to address boulevard plowing, noting no action could be taken at this meeting as it was agendized for discussion only.

### Item 5B: Review of 2027–2030 Infrastructure and Facility Projects
City staff presented the five-year capital infrastructure plan showing approximately $27.4 million in projected borrowing over five years for 2027. Hoenecke asked about the status of grant funding in the plan; staff clarified that $600,000 of the $900,000 in anticipated 2027 grants had been tied to the solar array project (which the finance committee had rejected and would likely be removed at council) and $300,000 was a state reimbursement for East Wausau Avenue real estate acquisition. Staff agreed to provide a breakdown distinguishing legally obligated projects from discretionary ones, and to add justification narratives to each project listing so constituents and council members can understand why each project is being pursued.

### Item 6: Committee Definition and Duties
The committee reviewed a memo from Assistant City Attorney Bonino outlining the committee's defined duties, including recommending purchase, sale, or lease of city real estate and disposition of vacant or underutilized city-owned property. Hoenecke noted that economic development and infrastructure committees have overlapping authority over real estate transactions and suggested a joint meeting of the two committees to clarify boundaries; Trueblood noted the mayor had requested this review months prior. Wiskowski asked that a future agenda item include an overview of underutilized and vacant city-owned properties, including how long the city has held them and any reasons they remain unused; staff noted Director Frick may already have compiled similar data for the economic development committee.

## Public Comment
Seventeen members of the public spoke. A resident of Elwood Boulevard who has lived there approximately 30 years described how the change to curb-side plowing has eliminated safe on-street parking and created bottlenecks, impeding garbage trucks, delivery vehicles, and potentially emergency responders, and asked the committee to return to the historical practice. Lou Larson, Alderperson District 10, speaking as a resident of South 21st Place, stated that the city attorney's office confirmed a state statute permits plows to operate against traffic with proper lighting and that the city's insurer would cover incidents under that practice, and argued the $40,000 equipment estimate was misleading. A resident of Highland Park Boulevard described photos she distributed showing plows passing approximately 7–8 feet from the curb, burying a sand barrel she relies on to sand a dangerous intersection herself, and asked why plows cannot plow closer to the curb. A resident of Highland Park Boulevard described cost and safety concerns including driveways blocked by ice, difficulty for elderly residents to clear snow, and potential inability of ambulances to access driveways. A resident of Oaklair Boulevard described the unique challenges of the first block of Oaklair Boulevard with two center boulevard breaks, the inability of vehicles including delivery trucks and emergency vehicles to pass when cars are parked, and dangerous sight-line obstructions from high snow banks. Carol Lukens, Alderperson District 1, speaking as a resident of Ethel Street, stated she reviewed the December 2023 SISM meeting and January 2024 council vote and confirmed the specific boulevard plowing change was never presented as a distinct item for council approval, and corroborated the legal finding about the Wisconsin statute allowing plowing against traffic with proper lighting. A resident of Highland Park Boulevard described the unique geography of that street — a hill sweeping past their driveway with Highland Court intersecting — and said the street had been narrowed during reconstruction, leaving no room for pedestrians who must walk in the road because boulevard sidewalks are not cleared in winter, and pleaded for a return to center plowing. A resident of Everest Boulevard who has lived there about three years described a 31-inch snowfall that left 7–8-foot snow walls in driveways, described neighbors including elderly residents and a person using a wheelchair who faced serious access and safety concerns, and said his wife had to pray when pulling out of the driveway because visibility was blocked. A resident of Highland Park Boulevard presented a detailed data analysis document, which he said he had sent to committee members, arguing that returning to center-boulevard plowing would save tens of thousands of dollars annually — estimating the cost of an extra plowing pass at about $2,500 per season for both neighborhoods — compared to the unbudgeted cost of $17,000–$31,000 per year to clear median sidewalks as required by city code, and recommended a formal amendment to municipal code to codify the historical practice. A resident of Everest Boulevard expressed confusion about why the matter had been repeatedly delayed, questioned why the city's cost estimate of $40,000 lacked supporting invoices or data-driven figures, and said the situation seemed inexplicable given that it was safer and fiscally sounder to return to the old policy. A resident of Oaklair Boulevard who has lived there 51 years noted the historical practice of center plowing dating back to at least the 1950s according to a longtime neighbor, and said she did not consider returning to that practice preferential treatment, drawing comparisons to plowing practices on Grand Avenue and Kent Street that exist for safety reasons. A resident of Oaklair Boulevard noted that before street reconstruction, fire hydrants were located in center boulevards and have since been moved curbside, meaning curb-side plowing now buries them and makes it harder for residents with hydrants on their property to keep them clear. A resident of Highland Park Boulevard noted the neighborhood's historic district status and said the boulevards were designed as a distinctive feature that warranted appropriate plowing treatment, and questioned why recreational bike trails were plowed if streets used daily were not being plowed in the most effective manner. A resident spoke to express support for neighbors, including elderly and disabled residents, and described personally damaging their car and missing work due to inability to exit their driveway after plowing. A resident of Oaklair Boulevard described the specific challenges of a driveway at a center boulevard break, including inability to place a garbage can near the curb and lack of a buffer zone when backing out into the street. A resident of Highland Park Boulevard echoed the call to return to center plowing, noting she had seen it done with plow blades without driving in reverse, and questioned why it could not still be done. A resident of Oaklair Boulevard alleged that a city official had falsely stated that driving a snowplow against traffic was illegal; the chair directed the speaker to address the substance of the alleged false information rather than characterize the official personally, and the speaker stated the false information was the claim that it is illegal to drive a snowplow the wrong way on a street.

## Action Items
- City attorney to provide a formal, researched legal opinion on the Wisconsin statute permitting plowing against traffic, including any signage or safety device requirements, driver liability questions, and insurance coverage, to be brought to a future meeting
- City staff (Director Lynman) to research the history of when and why the direction was given to close streets before plowing against traffic, and who issued that direction
- City staff to return to a future meeting with multiple costed options for addressing boulevard snow plowing, presented in options format with implementation details
- Staff to provide a breakdown of the 2027–2030 capital project list distinguishing legally obligated projects from discretionary ones
- Staff to add justification narratives to each project in the capital infrastructure plan
- A future agenda item to include an overview of underutilized and vacant city-owned properties, including duration of ownership and reasons for non-use; staff to coordinate with Director Frick who may have existing data
- Boulevard landscape planting subcommittee to be established and to work in coordination with the sustainability commission
- City attorney's office to review and potentially clarify the language defining the duties of the Infrastructure and Facilities Committee versus the Economic Development Committee, potentially leading to a joint meeting of the two committees
