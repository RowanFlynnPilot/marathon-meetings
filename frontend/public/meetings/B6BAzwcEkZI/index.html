The Wausau Finance Committee met on Tuesday, August 25, 2026, approving three action items: creation of a temporary planning intern position for the comprehensive plan update, reclassification of an HR specialist to HR generalist (with one dissenting vote), and establishment of 2026 budget calendar dates. The committee also approved a tax increment revenue bond for the Foundry on Third development, received a detailed bond sale report from financial adviser Ehlers, and held extensive discussions on funding gaps for lead service line replacement and a presentation on equalized and assessed values and their role in the city budget — matters with significant financial implications for residents.

Approval of Minutes – August 12, 2026

The minutes were approved without discussion. Martens noted a motion from Neal and a second from Tierney; the item carried on a voice vote.

Approval of Creation of Planning Intern Position

Planning staff member Carrie Edmonson presented the request for a temporary part-time planning intern to assist with the comprehensive plan update's public engagement efforts, noting the position would be funded through an already-approved capital request. Alderperson Watson asked about the funding source and confirmed no additional budget appropriation was needed. Alderperson Hoenecke asked about the target candidate pool (likely UWSP land resources students) and raised a concern about interns handling difficult resident interactions; Edmonson clarified the intern would never conduct public engagement alone but always alongside experienced staff. Alderperson Neal asked about desired skill sets; Edmonson emphasized community engagement interest and planning knowledge. The item had previously passed unanimously at the HR committee. The motion was made by Tierney and seconded by Watson; it carried unanimously.

Reclassification of HR Specialist to HR Generalist

Interim HR Director Ann Keenan presented the reclassification, explaining the current specialist is already performing all generalist duties including apprentice hiring, police recruitment, ADA compliance, FMLA, and workers' comp work, and has maxed out the specialist pay band at approximately $62,000. Martens emphasized the equity issue of paying specialist wages for generalist work, drawing a comparison to pay parity practices in police and fire. Hoenecke expressed concern that the proposed pay range exceeded local market data he found on Indeed and Glassdoor, suggesting the $50,000–$60,000 range was competitive, though Keenan noted Marathon County's generalist starts in the low $60s and reaches the high $70s. The item had passed unanimously at the HR committee. The motion was made by Neal and seconded by Tierney; it was approved with one dissenting vote from Hoenecke.

Establishing 2026 Budget Calendar Dates

Finance Director Maryann (last name not clearly stated in transcript) presented proposed budget workshop dates starting at 6:30 p.m. to avoid conflicts with other committee meetings. Tierney stated she would not participate unless budget books were provided with ample review time prior to the October 5th start date. The motion was made by Hoenecke and seconded by Watson; it carried, with Tierney not voting.

Approval of Foundry on Third Tax Increment Revenue Bond

Economic development staff member Patrick explained that the municipal revenue obligation bond is required by the development agreement with T-Wall for the Foundry on Third project to give the developer assurance of TIF payments, particularly since the district's expenditure period ends before the mandated termination date. Martens and staff clarified the bond is funded through TID 12 tax increment — not the general fund — and does not add to the city's general indebtedness. Hoenecke asked about ongoing developer obligations, and staff confirmed the developer is meeting minimum construction cost and assessed value requirements in the agreement; the assessed value rose from approximately $7.1 million to $33.6 million. Watson and Tierney asked about the tax revenue currently generated; staff indicated the first meaningful payment would occur based on the January 1, 2026 assessment. Hoenecke also raised a hypothetical about early TID closure; finance staff noted that closing a district before debt is retired would require funding the remainder solely from the city's share of taxes. The motion was made by Hoenecke and seconded by Watson; it carried unanimously.

Options to Pay for Private-Side Lead Service Line Replacement

City Engineer Eric (last name not stated in transcript) presented three options for covering the funding gap for private-side lead service line replacement as state and federal forgivable loan funding decreases. This was a discussion-only item. Hoenecke raised equity concerns about residents in the second half of the replacement program potentially paying costs that earlier residents did not, and estimated that a $2,000 city subsidy per line across roughly 1,000 remaining lines would equal $2 million. Watson suggested income-based or property-value-based subsidies rather than a flat dollar amount, and asked about structuring subsidies as a percentage of cost rather than a fixed figure given unpredictable construction costs. Neal suggested accelerating the replacement program in 2027 to take advantage of the 0.25% interest loan before it expires. Staff discussed using special assessments spread over 10 years, TID project plan amendments as a possible partial funding source, and economies of scale with contractor Community Infrastructure Partners. Staff noted the city is roughly halfway through replacement and noted the federal deadline has been extended to 2038. No action was taken; the committee directed continued discussion during the upcoming budget process.

Presentation from Ehlers Regarding 2026A Promissory Note Sale

Brian Rorr from Ehlers presented the results of the June 2026 general obligation promissory note sale of $10,560,000. Moody's affirmed the Aa3 rating; the city received seven bids — described as extraordinary — with a low spread of 17 basis points between high and low bids, and an approximate 3.1% interest rate. The sale closed July 9th. The estimated taxpayer impact for a $350,000 property is approximately $6 more in the first year of new debt service, with decreasing levy impact in subsequent years; the total 10–20-year cost of new debt to that sample taxpayer is approximately $674. The city's general obligation debt now stands at approximately 31% of its statutory 5% equalized value limit, with $164 million in residual capacity. Hoenecke asked about debt per capita comparisons and sustainability of annual borrowing levels; Rorr said Moody's views the city's position as healthy and suggested focusing on the ability to filter new debt into a flat tax impact. Finance staff noted that several TID-related projects are included in the borrowing and that future borrowing needs will likely decrease as TID project plans wind down. Staff also clarified the flexibility of promissory notes versus bonds for reallocating funds among projects.

Presentation on Equalized and Assessed Valuation and Role Within the Budget

Finance Director Maryann gave an educational presentation explaining the dual-value assessment system in Wisconsin, the role of equalized value in levy limits, TID creation caps, and general obligation debt limits. She noted that the city's TID percentage of equalized value has dropped to 8.34% — well below the 12% statutory cap — following recent TID closures, restoring flexibility to create or amend districts. She explained that the levy limit tied to net new construction (2.544% for the upcoming budget cycle) severely constrains the city's ability to grow revenue, and that the $1.3 million expenditure restraint grant provides a major incentive for fiscal discipline. She highlighted that the city has no OPEB (Other Post-Employment Benefits) liability compared to peers, while noting the environmental cleanup fund has declined from $1.4 million in 2021 to approximately $600,000 and will eventually require annual budgeting when depleted. Watson asked about PFAs settlement funds; finance staff confirmed approximately $3.4 million was received last year plus ongoing structured settlement payments, with no earmarking yet determined.

Presentation on 2025 Financial Results

The committee opted not to go through the full PowerPoint slide by slide given the late hour and the overlap with earlier presentations. Neal asked Maryann for her bottom-line assessment of the city's fiscal health; she noted the city is in the middle of the pack on general obligation debt compared to peer communities but high on revenue bond debt due to the new water and wastewater plants, while emphasizing the absence of OPEB liability as a significant positive differentiator. Martens noted the revenue bond debt position is a moving window as other communities will need to undertake similar infrastructure upgrades. Hoenecke asked whether the auditor presentation would come to Finance Committee; staff confirmed it would. Watson asked about the housing stock improvement fund increase, which staff confirmed was TID-related. The full presentation will be revisited during budget sessions.
