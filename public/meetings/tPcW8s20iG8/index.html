The Wausau City Council met on September 8, 2026, issuing three proclamations, hearing a sustainability committee update, and taking several votes including a contentious debate over a lead service line replacement ordinance. The most significant action was passage of the lead line ordinance (file 260921) after a lengthy debate over whether residents would be forced to pay for replacements, while a resolution approving a permanent billboard easement at 1514 North Third Street was unanimously rejected.

Discussion

Proclamations

Mayor Doug Diny read three proclamations: declaring September as Ovarian Cancer Awareness Month, declaring September 2026 as National Suicide Prevention and Action Month (citing North Central Health Care and Hope for the Day as local partners), and declaring September 17–23 as Constitution Week marking the 239th anniversary of the U.S. Constitution. A representative from the Daughters of the American Revolution accepted the Constitution Week proclamation and encouraged residents to visit Yonke Bookstore and Marathon County Public Library.

Sustainability, Energy, and Environment Committee Presentation

Committee representative Jesse Karns presented an update on the committee's work toward a five-year sustainability action plan, expected to be finalized and presented to council in April 2027. Karns highlighted a community survey launched September 1 and running through October 31, outreach at Artfest, a composting demonstration partnership with SUS, a Wausau Sustainability Award to be presented during Earth Week, and the city's 2023 resolution to achieve 100% clean energy for city government by 2050. Alder Lukans encouraged alders to provide input to the committee and noted the direct community benefits of the committee's work.

File 06909: Perpetual Easement for Lamar Advertising Signs at 1514 North Third Street

The resolution came to the council after being voted down unanimously in the Infrastructure and Facilities Committee. Alders Slansky, Neil, Trueblood, and Martins all spoke against it, arguing that a permanent easement would limit future redevelopment flexibility on a parcel where active negotiations are reportedly underway. Martins noted the city has owned the property since 2006 and that outdoor advertising is scarce, making a continued lease more advantageous. The motion failed 0-11.

File 06906: DPW Organizational Structure Changes and Succession Planning

Public Works Director Eric Lynman was invited to the podium by Council President Terry Kilian, who asked him to confirm on the record that proposed utility fee increases were not a dependency of this reorganization proposal — Lynman confirmed the changes are within the current budget. Alder Watson noted the reorganization appeared to be ahead of a pending broader organizational study. Alder Heni asked about whether elimination of a part-time facilities position would affect winter plowing capacity; Lynman confirmed plowing remains the top priority. Alder Larson asked how the changes would benefit citizens and whether the Murphy Report had informed them; Lynman said some Mercury Report suggestions had already been implemented. Mayor Diny noted succession planning is a goal for all nine department directors. The motion passed 10-1, with Alder Larson indicating he would withhold his vote pending a city audit.

File 260918: Reclassification of HR Specialist to HR Generalist

Multiple alders spoke in strong support, with Wiscowski, Slansky, Tyranny, Martins, and Larson all noting the employee has long been performing generalist-level duties while paid at the specialist rate, and that the HR department is critically understaffed — described as currently a department of two while structured for four. Alder Trueblood raised a concern that while the $40,760 in savings covers the reclassification cost in 2026, it will be a permanent ongoing budget impact in future years. Alder Heni asked for clarification on the precise salary amount, which the interim HR director confirmed as the resolution amount. The motion passed 11-0.

File 260921: Lead and Galvanized Water Service Line Replacement Ordinance

This item generated the longest and most contentious debate of the evening. DPW Director Eric Lynman explained the city has replaced over 500 lines in 2024, 1,500 in 2025, and is on track for 1,700 in 2026, with an EPA deadline of 2037. Council President Kilian said she would not support it because residents were initially told there would be no fees. Alder Slansky supported it to qualify for future grants but asked that costs be spread equitably. Alder Trueblood drew a contrast with the evening's pending $28 million Riverlife development agreement and argued the city had overpromised on the no-cost pledge. Alder Watson raised questions about circular logic between the ordinance and funding, and asked whether enforcement could be tied to funding availability. Alder Heni moved to amend the ordinance to prohibit penalties, enforcement, and water disconnection until the council adopts a financing program defining maximum obligations, repayment terms, and hardship considerations; Alder Tyranny seconded. After extended debate on whether that amendment could jeopardize PSC approval or grant eligibility, Heni withdrew the amendment without objection. Alder Larson called the question to end debate on the main motion. The ordinance ultimately passed, though the exact tally was not clearly stated in the transcript before the recording ended.
